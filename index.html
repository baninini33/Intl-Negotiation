Numbers to know (the book's own counts and lists)

Ch 1. Definition (p. 20): non-violent, voluntary, two or more interdependent actors, share a fixed value or create a new one, agreement all sides can accept. Three levels: negotiation, international negotiation, diplomatic negotiation (sovereign states only). Iklé: four main types (normalization, redistribution, extension, value-adding) plus negotiation without agreements (pp. 28 to 31). Five fields: economic, political, security, regulatory, administrative (p. 33). Forms (pp. 34 to 35): direct, third party, written exchange, phone, online, lobbying. Five approaches (p. 37): process (linear, circular, phase), structural realism, decision analysis, behavior analysis, integrative.

Ch 2. Top line = maximum interests, public. Bottom line = lowest acceptable, hidden. Bargaining range = between the two top lines. Acceptance zone = between one party's own top and bottom line. ZOPA = overlap of the two zones (positive bargaining). GAP = distance between the two bottom lines when the zones do not overlap (negative bargaining). Position = what is required, interest = why. BATNA is the main source of negotiating power (pp. 48 to 56).

Ch 3. Objective: balance of power, internal context, international environment. Subjective: culture, psychology, skills (p. 61). Textbook labels: Level I = national (domestic interest groups), Level II = international (p. 65). Multipolar order is the more favourable one (p. 70).

Ch 6. Strategy is built on three bases: game theory, nature of the negotiation (competitive or cooperative), concern level (p. 123). Games: minimax, sequential, simultaneous (Prisoner's Dilemma, Chicken), Nash equilibrium. Concern depends on acceptance-zone width and BATNA strength (p. 137).

Ch 7. Five stages: preparation, pre-negotiation, formal negotiation, negotiation closure, implementation (p. 143). Master plan: eight sections (p. 147). Formal negotiation: opening, principled, detailed (p. 154). Detailed-stage skills: style, questions, presentations, managing extreme emotions (p. 156).

Ch 10. Three characteristics: diversity and complexity, decision-making rules, psychosocial dynamics (p. 207). Three strategies: coalitions, rules, roles (p. 211). Four decision forms: majority, contribution-based, consensus, hybrid (p. 213). Three roles: positively pro-active, negatively pro-active, passive (p. 214). Five stages (p. 215). Four agreement forms: statement, resolution, recommendations and conclusions, decision (p. 220). Trade-off is the most important tactic (p. 221).

Ch 11. Four levels: good offices, facilitation, conciliation, mediation (p. 230). Mediators: states, international organizations, NGOs, individuals. Objective factors: balance of power, willingness, timing, context (pp. 237 to 238). Three strategies: facilitating, formulating, manipulating (p. 240). Four stages: preparation, pre-mediation, formal mediation, post-mediation (p. 244).

Predicted question types and how to beat them

Definition. One term, one sentence. Learn the book's own wording (strategy, minimax, Nash equilibrium, good offices).

List. "Which list gives the N ...". The trap is a near-identical list with one item swapped or the order changed, so memorize counts first, then items.

Contrast. Two close terms: position vs interest, positive vs negative leverage, mediation vs arbitration, natural vs issue-specific coalition.

Not/Except. Three options are true, one is not. Check each against the book's list.

Statement. "Which statement is correct". Watch absolute words (always, only, never).

Why / Case. The book's reason for a rule, and its named examples (Paris talks, Singapore and Vietnam as good offices, Sant-Egidio, Algeria).

Scenario. A short story, name the concept. Match it to the book's definition, not to your own intuition.

Numbers. Draw the line. Top lines give the range, each side's top to bottom gives its zone, the overlap is the ZOPA, no overlap means a GAP between the bottom lines. Votes: simple majority is over 50 percent, important issues need two-thirds.

Best move / Diagnose. Pick the action the book advises, or the outcome it says follows.

Book vs lecture: where they differ (follow the book if a question quotes it)

Forms. Book: lobbying is a form of negotiation, six forms (pp. 34 to 35). Lecture: lobbying is a skill, five forms.

Approaches. Book: five major approaches (p. 37). Lecture: four approaches, five models.

BATNA. Book: a strong BATNA should be communicated, only a weak one hidden (p. 56).

Iklé. Book: four main types plus negotiation without agreements with six purposes (pp. 28 to 31).

Two-level game. Book: Level I national, Level II international (p. 65). Putnam and the lecture reverse it. Read the question's own definition.

Power. Book: tangible and intangible elements (p. 61). Lecture: hard, soft, network power.

Pre-negotiation. Book: negotiating about negotiation topics, not an actual negotiation (p. 148). Paris venue offered 2 May 1968 (p. 152).

Skills. Book (p. 156): style, questions, presentations, managing extreme emotions. Lecture: listening, questions, presenting, arguing.

Multilateral. Book: three roles (p. 214), four decision forms (p. 213), three characteristics (p. 207). Lecture: five roles, five rules, four characteristics.

Mediation. Book cites Article 36 of the UN Charter (p. 234), names stage three "formal mediation" (p. 244), and describes ripe time as exhaustion with no alternatives, impasse where neither side can win (pp. 237 to 238).
